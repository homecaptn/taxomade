Applying for a visa in a foreign country involves navigating a maze of bureaucracy. The Greek Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) is an incredible opportunity, but the application process requires precision. A single missing stamp or misinterpreted requirement can lead to months of delays or an outright rejection.

Here are the top 5 most common mistakes applicants make—and how to avoid them.

## 1. Submitting Insurance That Doesn't Match the Consulate's Requirements

A common reason for delays.

The consulate wants a policy that covers medical care and hospitalisation in Greece for the entire visa period. What exactly is accepted differs from consulate to consulate: some accept international nomad policies, others want more. A policy limited to travel emergencies is usually not enough. Read your consulate's wording before you buy, and bring the policy document, not just a certificate.

## 2. Misunderstanding the €3,500 Income Requirement

The financial threshold for the DNV is €3,500 per month. A critical mistake is assuming this refers to your gross salary. 

For employees, the law explicitly requires **€3,500 net after tax**. If your gross salary is €4,000 but your take-home pay is €3,200, you don't meet the threshold. For freelancers, the law speaks of a stable income of €3,500 shown through contracts and bank statements. Either way, make sure your documents show the figure clearly and consistently.

## 3. Attempting to Apply Locally on a Tourist Visa

> [!WARNING]
> This route is closed since February 2026.

Before 2026, it was a common strategy to fly to Athens on a 90-day Schengen stay and apply for the digital nomad residence permit from within the country, to avoid dealing with the consulate back home.

Since Law 5275/2026 (Government Gazette A' 17, 6 February 2026), that in-country route is closed. You apply for the national (Type-D) digital nomad visa at the Greek consulate in your country of residence *before* you travel. Confirm the current procedure with your consulate.

## 4. Missing Apostilles and Incorrect Translations

The Greek bureaucracy requires certainty that your foreign documents are genuine. Simply printing out a background check or marriage certificate is not enough.

Official documents (especially Criminal Record Certificates and civil registry documents) must bear an **Apostille stamp**—an international certification under the Hague Convention. 

Furthermore, these documents must be officially translated into Greek, by a translator from Greece's register of certified translators, a Greek lawyer, or the consulate itself. An uncertified translation is not accepted.

## 5. Ignoring the 183-Day Tax Rule

Many nomads apply for the DNV focused entirely on the lifestyle and completely ignore the tax implications until it's too late.

If you spend more than 183 days in Greece within any 12-month period, you generally become a **Greek tax resident**, and from your first day in the country. Failing to plan for this can lead to nasty surprises, including double taxation or fines for failing to declare global income in Greece.

One thing the DNV does not give you: the **Article 5C 50% tax break**. It requires a job with a Greek employer or a Greek business, and the visa allows neither. Plan for full Greek taxation of your worldwide income once you become resident, with relief under the double tax treaty between Greece and your home country. See [why the DNV and 5C don't combine](/guides/5c-vs-digital-nomad-visa).

## Summary

The Greek DNV process is manageable, but it demands attention to detail. By securing insurance that matches your consulate's requirements, proving your income accurately, applying from your home country, getting your apostilles in order, and planning your tax strategy, you can ensure a smooth transition to your new life in the Mediterranean.
