The Greek Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) is straightforward if you get the details right. Most delays and refusals come from a handful of avoidable mistakes. Here are the five we see most often, and how to avoid them. For the full process, see our [Digital Nomad Visa guide](/guides/ultimate-guide-dnv-greece).

## The short answer

- **Apply at the consulate** in your country of residence before you travel; the in-country route is no longer used since Law 5275/2026.
- **Income:** €3,500 a month **net**, for employees and freelancers alike (+20% spouse, +15% per child).
- **Documents:** insurance covering medical care in Greece, apostilles and certified Greek translations.
- **Work only for employers or clients abroad**, never Greek ones.
- **Tax:** after more than 183 days you are usually Greek tax resident, and the DNV gives no access to the 5C tax break.

## 1. Planning to apply from inside Greece

Until early 2026, many applicants entered on a 90-day Schengen stay and applied for the digital nomad residence permit inside Greece. Since [Law 5275/2026](https://www.taxheaven.gr/law/5275/2026) (Government Gazette A' 17, 6 February 2026), that route is no longer available in practice: you apply for the national (Type-D) visa at the Greek consulate in your country of residence before you travel. Confirm the current procedure with your consulate.

The Type-D visa is valid for up to 12 months. Once in Greece, apply for the two-year, renewable residence permit (type I.8) before the visa expires. Fees are €75 for the visa and €1,000 for the permit. More on the difference to a short stay in [DNV vs tourist visa](/guides/dnv-vs-tourist-visa).

## 2. Misunderstanding the €3,500 income requirement

The law sets **€3,500 a month net, after the taxes due in the country where you work**, for employment and freelance (service or contract) income alike ([Article 68 of Law 5038/2023](https://www.taxheaven.gr/law/5038/2023/arthro/68)). If your gross is €4,000 but your take-home pay is €3,200, you don't meet it.

With family the threshold rises by 20% for a spouse or partner (€4,200 in total) and 15% per child. Show the figure consistently in contracts, payslips or invoices, and bank statements.

## 3. Documents that don't meet the consulate's requirements

**Insurance.** The consulate wants a policy that covers medical care and hospital treatment in Greece for the whole visa period. What is accepted differs between consulates: some accept international nomad policies, others want more. A policy limited to travel emergencies is usually not enough. Read your consulate's wording before you buy, and bring the policy document, not just a certificate.

**Apostilles and translations.** Official documents, especially criminal record certificates and civil registry documents, need an apostille under the Hague Convention. They must also be translated into Greek by a translator from Greece's register of certified translators, a Greek lawyer, or the consulate itself. Requirements vary by consulate; an uncertified translation is not accepted.

## 4. Working for Greek clients or employers

The DNV is for work for employers or clients outside Greece. You sign a declaration that you won't provide work or services to an employer based in Greece, and the permit gives you no right to run a business here. A local job, a Greek client or freelance work on the side breaches the terms of your permit. Family members on your permit may not work in Greece at all.

## 5. Treating the DNV as tax-free

If you spend more than 183 days in Greece within any 12-month period, or your home and centre of life move here, you generally become a Greek tax resident, counted from your first day in the country (see [the 183-day rule](/guides/tax-residency-greece-183-days)). The exception for stays purely for tourism or medical reasons does not cover people living and working here on a DNV.

From then on you need an [AFM](/guides/what-is-greek-afm-number) and file an annual Greek return on your worldwide income at the normal rates of 9% to 44%. Pay for work you physically do in Greece is usually taxed here first; if another country also taxes it, the double tax treaty (where one exists) decides how relief is given. Penalties for missed filings come automatically.

One thing the DNV does not give you: the **Article 5C 50% tax break**. It requires a job with a Greek employer or a Greek business, and the visa allows neither. See [why the DNV and 5C don't combine](/guides/5c-vs-digital-nomad-visa).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I apply for the Greek Digital Nomad Visa from inside Greece?

Since Law 5275/2026, applications go through a Greek consulate or embassy before you travel; the earlier in-country route via a Schengen stay is no longer used in practice. Confirm the current procedure with your consulate.

### Is the €3,500 DNV income requirement gross or net?

Net. For employment and freelance income alike, the law refers to €3,500 a month after the taxes due in the country where you work, plus 20% for a spouse or partner and 15% per child.

### Can I work for Greek clients on a Digital Nomad Visa?

No. The DNV is for work for employers or clients outside Greece; you declare that you won't work for an employer based in Greece, and the permit doesn't allow business activity here.

### Do digital nomads pay tax in Greece?

Yes, once you become Greek tax resident: main home or centre of life in Greece, or more than 183 days within any 12 months. You then declare your worldwide income in an annual Greek return, with relief under the tax treaty with your home country.

### Can Digital Nomad Visa holders use the 50% tax break (Article 5C)?

No. The DNV does not allow work for Greek employers or clients or business activity in Greece, which is exactly what Article 5C requires.
