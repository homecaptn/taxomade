One of the most intimidating aspects of moving abroad is understanding where, when, and how much tax you owe. For remote workers moving to Greece, the rules can seem complex, but they generally revolve around one core concept: the 183-day rule.

Here is a simplified guide to understanding tax residency in Greece.

## 1. The 183-Day Rule Explained

Global tax law relies on the concept of tax residency to determine which country has the primary right to tax your income. 

In Greece, you are generally considered a Greek tax resident if you spend **more than 183 days** in the country during any 12-month period. This presence does not have to be continuous; the days are cumulative. 

- **Under 183 Days (Less than 6 months):** If you spend a few months in Greece over the summer and then leave, you generally remain a tax resident of your home country. You do not owe Greek taxes on your foreign-earned income.
- **Over 183 Days (More than 6 months):** If you exceed this threshold, Greece considers you a tax resident. This means you are legally obligated to declare and pay tax on your **worldwide income** to the Greek tax authorities.

## 2. Does an AFM Make You a Tax Resident?

A very common fear among expats is that applying for a Greek Tax Number (AFM) instantly makes them liable for Greek taxes.

**This is a myth.**

An AFM is simply an identification number. You need it to buy a car, rent a long-term apartment, or open a bank account. Holding an AFM does not automatically make you a tax resident, nor does it trigger tax liabilities on its own. Your tax residency is triggered by your physical presence (the 183-day rule) and where your "center of vital interests" (family, primary business) lies.

## 3. Avoiding Double Taxation

"If I become a Greek tax resident, will I be taxed by Greece AND my home country?"

To prevent expats from paying tax twice on the same income, Greece has signed **Double Taxation Treaties (DTTs)** with dozens of countries, including the UK, Canada, Australia, and most of the EU.

Under these treaties, if you are taxed in Greece, you can usually claim a tax credit in your home country, or vice versa, ensuring you only pay the higher of the two tax rates, rather than both combined.

### The US Exception
United States citizens face a unique challenge: the US taxes based on *citizenship*, not just residency. If you are a US citizen living in Greece, you must still file US tax returns declaring your worldwide income. However, thanks to the US-Greece Double Taxation Treaty and IRS provisions like the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) or Foreign Tax Credits, most US expats in Greece do not end up paying double tax. You will, however, need an accountant familiar with both systems to file the paperwork correctly.

## 4. The Benefit of Becoming a Greek Tax Resident

While avoiding Greek taxes might seem appealing, becoming a Greek tax resident is actually the gateway to massive savings for remote workers.

If you intentionally become a tax resident and register your business locally, you unlock the ability to apply for **Article 5C**. This government incentive allows qualifying expats to pay zero income tax on 50% of their earnings for seven years. 

For high-earning digital nomads, structuring your life to *become* a Greek tax resident under this scheme is often far more profitable than remaining a tax resident in high-tax countries like Germany, the UK, or the US.

## Summary

Do not let the fear of taxation prevent you from enjoying Greece. Track your days, understand that an AFM is just an ID number, and remember that with proper planning, Greek tax residency can actually be a financial blessing rather than a burden.
