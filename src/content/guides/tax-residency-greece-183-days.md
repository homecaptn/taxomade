One of the most intimidating aspects of moving abroad is understanding where, when, and how much tax you owe. For remote workers moving to Greece, the rules generally revolve around one core concept: tax residency, and the 183-day rule behind it.

Here is a simplified guide to understanding tax residency in Greece.

## The short answer

- **More than 183 days** in Greece in any 12-month period makes you tax resident, from your first day.
- **Fewer days can still count** if your main home or centre of vital interests is here.
- **Residents are taxed on worldwide income**; non-residents only on Greek-source income.
- **An AFM does not make you resident.**
- **Treaties with 58 countries** prevent most double taxation; US citizens file in both countries regardless.
- **New residents who work here** may qualify for the 50% Article 5C exemption.

## 1. The 183-Day Rule Explained

Global tax law relies on the concept of tax residency to determine which country has the primary right to tax your income.

Under [Article 4 of the Greek Income Tax Code (Law 4172/2013)](https://aade.gr/en/greeks-abroad-non-residents/income-taxation/tax-residence-natural-persons-itc), you are a Greek tax resident if you are physically present in Greece for **more than 183 days, cumulatively, in any 12-month period**. The days do not have to be continuous, the period is not tied to the calendar year, and once you cross the threshold you count as resident **from the first day of your presence**.

- **Under 183 Days:** If you spend a few months in Greece over the summer and then leave, you generally remain a tax resident of your home country. Greece then taxes only Greek-source income, not your foreign earnings.
- **Over 183 Days:** Greece considers you a tax resident. You are legally obligated to declare and pay tax on your **worldwide income** to the Greek tax authorities.

Two caveats. Days are not the only test: you are also resident if your main home, habitual abode or **centre of vital interests** (family, main business) is in Greece, even with fewer than 183 days. And the day count does not apply to stays of up to 365 days for purely tourist or medical reasons; working remotely from a Greek apartment is not tourism in this sense.

## 2. Does an AFM Make You a Tax Resident?

A very common fear among expats is that applying for a Greek Tax Number (AFM) instantly makes them liable for Greek taxes.

**This is a myth.**

An AFM is simply an identification number. You need it to buy a car, rent a long-term apartment, or open a bank account. Holding an AFM does not automatically make you a tax resident, nor does it trigger tax liabilities on its own. Your tax residency is determined by your physical presence (the 183-day rule) and where your centre of vital interests lies. Just make sure you are registered as a non-resident until you actually move. More in our guide on [what the AFM is and how to get one](/guides/what-is-greek-afm-number).

## 3. Avoiding Double Taxation

"If I become a Greek tax resident, will I be taxed by Greece AND my home country?"

To prevent expats from paying tax twice on the same income, Greece has [double taxation treaties with 58 countries](https://www.aade.gr/en/citizens/texts-contracts-treaties-avoidance-double-taxation-greece), including the UK, the US, Canada, Germany and every EU member state except Sweden, which terminated its treaty with Greece from 2022. There is **no treaty with Australia** yet; the two countries are negotiating one, but nothing has been signed.

Under these treaties, tax paid in Greece is usually credited in your home country, or vice versa, or the income is exempt in one of the two countries, so in most cases you do not pay full tax twice. If both countries claim you as resident, the treaty tie-breaker decides: permanent home, then centre of vital interests, then habitual abode, then nationality.

### The US Exception
United States citizens face a unique challenge: the US taxes based on *citizenship*, not just residency. If you are a US citizen living in Greece, you must still file US tax returns declaring your worldwide income. The [US–Greece treaty dates from 1950](https://cpasforexpats.com/post/us-greece-tax-treaty) and has a saving clause, so it does not switch off US taxation. Double tax is avoided through IRS provisions instead: the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion ([$132,900 for tax year 2026](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2025/10/tnf-rev-proc-2025-32-inflation-adjustments-for-2026-individual-taxpayers.html)) or the Foreign Tax Credit for Greek tax paid. You will need an accountant familiar with both systems.

## 4. The Benefit of Becoming a Greek Tax Resident

While avoiding Greek taxes might seem appealing, becoming a Greek tax resident is actually the gateway to significant savings if you move your work here.

If you become a tax resident and take up employment with a Greek employer (or the Greek branch of a foreign company) or register as self-employed in Greece, you can apply for **[Article 5C](/guides/50-percent-tax-break-article-5c)**. This incentive exempts **50% of your Greek employment or business income** from income tax for **seven years**. The main conditions: you were not a Greek tax resident in at least five of the six years before the move, and you stay in Greece for at least two years. Digital Nomad Visa holders cannot use it, because the visa does not allow Greek employment or business activity.

For high earners, structuring your life to *become* a Greek tax resident under this scheme is often far more profitable than remaining a tax resident in high-tax countries like Germany or the UK. (US citizens stay subject to US tax regardless, see above.)

## 5. Leaving Greece Again

Residency works in both directions. If you later move away, you must formally transfer your tax residency abroad, and the deadline is tight: the application is due by the [last working day of the first ten days of March of the year after you leave](https://aade.gr/en/greeks-abroad-non-residents/income-taxation/transferring-tax-residency-greece-abroad), supporting documents (typically a foreign tax residency certificate) by early September. AADE still accepts late filings until 31 December of the year after you leave; until then, you risk being treated as resident.

## Summary

Do not let the fear of taxation prevent you from enjoying Greece. Track your days, understand that an AFM is just an ID number, and remember that with proper planning, Greek tax residency can actually be a financial blessing rather than a burden.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days can I stay in Greece without becoming tax resident?

Up to 183 days in any 12-month period, as long as your main home and centre of vital interests are not in Greece. Above 183 days you are tax resident from your first day of presence.

### Does getting an AFM make me a Greek tax resident?

No. The AFM is an identification number. Residency depends on your days in Greece and where your home and centre of vital interests are.

### Can I be tax resident in Greece with fewer than 183 days?

Yes. If your main home, habitual abode or centre of vital interests (family, main business) is in Greece, you can be resident regardless of the day count.

### Do US citizens living in Greece pay tax in both countries?

They file in both. The US taxes by citizenship, and the 1950 treaty does not stop that; double tax is avoided through the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion or the Foreign Tax Credit.

### How do I stop being a Greek tax resident when I leave?

Apply to transfer your tax residency abroad by the first ten days of March of the year after you leave, with a foreign tax residency certificate by early September. AADE accepts late filings until 31 December of the following year.
