You keep your job with a company in Berlin, London or New York and move to Greece. Same contract, same salary. For Greece, a lot changes: where you pay income tax, who pays social security, and what your employer suddenly owes the Greek system. This guide covers those mechanics for people who stay employed, not freelancers.

## The short answer

- **Tax residency:** more than 183 days in Greece within 12 months, or your main home here.
- **Income tax:** your salary is taxed in Greece (9% to 44% in 2026), because you work here. Your employer's country should stop taxing it.
- **Withholding:** a foreign employer without a Greek establishment withholds no Greek tax. You declare the salary in your E1 and pay yourself.
- **EFKA:** Greek social security usually applies: 13.37% employee and 21.79% employer in 2026. The employer registers with EFKA or lets you pay on its behalf.
- **Exceptions:** an A1 for a posting (normally up to 24 months), multi-country work, or a bilateral agreement for non-EU employers.
- **No advance tax** on salary, but a full year's tax arrives at once after your first return.
- **Article 5C** does not work with a purely foreign employer, and never for Digital Nomad Visa holders.

## When Greece starts taxing your salary

You become Greek tax resident with more than 183 days in Greece in any 12-month period, and then count as resident from your first day here. Your main home or family life being here can make you resident sooner. The details are in [the 183-day rule](/guides/tax-residency-greece-183-days/).

From then on, Greece taxes your worldwide income. Double tax treaties follow the OECD model: salary is taxable where the work is physically performed. A desk in Athens means Greece, wherever your employer is based, while days you work in your employer's country can still be taxed there. The treaties' short-stay exception protects people who are not Greek residents; it does not help someone who has moved here. US citizens remain taxable in the US because of their citizenship and offset the Greek tax with US credits ([moving to Greece from the US](/guides/moving-to-greece-from-us-tax-guide/)).

**The common trap:** your employer keeps running home-country payroll and withholding home-country tax. That tax is usually not owed once you are resident in Greece and work here, and Greece generally credits only foreign tax that the treaty allows the other country to charge. So you claim it back abroad, often through a refund procedure with a [Greek tax residence certificate](/guides/greek-tax-residency-certificate/), while paying Greek tax in full. Fix the payroll setup in the month you move, not at year end.

## Income tax: who withholds?

Greek payroll withholding is an obligation of payers with a presence in Greece. Under [Article 61 of the Income Tax Code](https://www.taxheaven.gr/law/4172/2013/arthro/61), a foreign company is a withholding agent only through a Greek permanent establishment. Without an entity, branch or PE here, it withholds nothing. That leaves two set-ups:

1. **No Greek withholding.** Your employer pays gross. You declare the salary in the [E1 tax return](/guides/greek-tax-return-e1-foreigners/) between 15 March and 15 July of the following year, as employment income from abroad, and Greece assesses the tax on the normal scale.
2. **Greek payroll through a provider or Employer of Record.** The employer appoints a Greek payroll provider, or moves you onto a Greek Employer of Record, which runs a Greek payslip with monthly withholding. Cleaner, but it costs a monthly fee, and an EoR becomes your legal employer.

### What the first assessment looks like

Greek advance tax (προκαταβολή φόρου) is calculated only on business income under [Article 69 of the Income Tax Code](https://www.taxheaven.gr/law/4172/2013/arthro/69). Salary is not. But without withholding, nothing has been paid during the year. On €60,000 of taxable salary (after employee EFKA; single, no children, over 30), the tax for one year is about €16,700 on the 2026 scale: the employee tax credit has fully phased out at that income. It is assessed in one go after you file. You can pay in eight monthly instalments from the end of July, or get the early-filing discount (4% if filed by 15 May, 3% by 15 June, 2% by 15 July) if you pay in full by the end of July.

Freelance side income does carry advance tax: 55% of the tax on it (halved in the first year), 50% from the tax year 2027 return under Law 5355/2026 (art. 12).

The e-payment rule applies too: spend at least 30% of your income (capped at €20,000 of spending) by card or electronic payment, or the shortfall is taxed at 22%.

## EFKA: social security for a foreign employer

### EU and EEA employers

[Regulation 883/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004R0883) (Art. 11) says you are insured where you physically work. A full-time remote employee living in Greece therefore falls under Greek EFKA, even though the employer is in Munich or Amsterdam. The EU framework agreement on cross-border telework, which lets part-time teleworkers stay in the employer's system, does not help: it never covered full-time remote work, and Greece has not signed it.

Under [Article 21 of Regulation 987/2009](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32009R0987), the foreign employer must then meet Greek obligations "as if" it were based in Greece: register with e-EFKA as an employer, file the monthly payroll declaration (APD) and pay contributions. Alternatively, employer and employee can agree that **the employee pays the contributions on the employer's behalf**; the employer notifies EFKA, and its underlying liability stays. Your contract should then say who bears the employer share.

In 2026 the private-sector rates are **13.37% employee and 21.79% employer**, on gross pay up to €7,761.94 a month. From 1 April 2027, [Law 5355/2026](https://www.taxheaven.gr/law/5355/2026) (art. 21) cuts the employee's unemployment contribution from 1.20% to 0.70%, bringing the employee rate to 12.87%. Employer costs are in [hiring your first employee in Greece](/guides/hiring-first-employee-greece/).

Three ways you can stay in your home system instead:

- **Posting (A1, Art. 12):** your employer sends you to Greece temporarily, normally for up to 24 months, and your home institution issues an A1. A permanent move you chose yourself is not a posting.
- **Multi-state work (Art. 13):** you work regularly in two or more EU countries and less than 25% of your working time is in Greece.
- **Art. 16 agreement:** an exception the two countries' authorities agree case by case.

With an A1, an S1 form gives you access to Greek public healthcare.

### Non-EU employers

The EU regulation does not apply. What matters is whether Greece has a social security agreement with the employer's country:

- **United States:** the [US–Greece agreement](https://www.ssa.gov/international/Agreement_Pamphlets/documents/Greece.pdf) (in force since 1994) keeps you in US Social Security if your US employer sends you for five years or less, with a certificate of coverage. If you were hired while already in Greece, Greek coverage applies.
- **Canada, Australia** and a few others have bilateral agreements with similar logic.
- **UK:** the social security protocol of the EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement follows rules close to the EU ones, including detachment for up to 24 months with a certificate.
- **No agreement:** Greek law applies to work done in Greece.

Most non-EU employers use a Greek payroll provider or an EoR rather than registering with EFKA themselves.

## Permanent establishment: the employer's risk

Greece applies the OECD rules through the treaties and Article 6 of the Income Tax Code. A home office can be a fixed place of business if the employer effectively requires you to work from it over a longer period, and an employee who habitually negotiates or signs contracts can create a dependent agent PE. Routine work without sales authority is lower risk. A PE means Greek corporate tax on the attributable profit and Greek withholding duties, so senior sales staff and managing directors should get this checked before moving.

## Article 5C and the Digital Nomad Visa

Article 5C exempts 50% of employment income for seven years if you were **not Greek tax resident in 5 of the previous 6 years** and move from an EU/EEA or tax-cooperation country. But the employment route requires a job with a **Greek legal entity or the Greek permanent establishment** of a foreign company. A purely foreign employer does not meet that, and AADE has published no guidance on Employer-of-Record set-ups. The details are in [5C for employees and remote workers](/guides/5c-for-eu-remote-workers/).

**Digital Nomad Visa holders are not 5C-eligible.** The visa forbids work for Greek employers and business activity in Greece, which is exactly what 5C requires ([why the DNV rules out 5C](/guides/5c-vs-digital-nomad-visa/)). Tax residency works as for everyone else: after more than 183 days, or with your main home here, you file Greek returns on worldwide income. The visa does not settle social security; the rules above still apply.

## Checklist before and after the move

1. **Agree the set-up with HR before you leave:** stop home-country withholding from your move date, choose Greek payroll, EoR or no Greek withholding, and settle EFKA (registration, employee-pays agreement or A1).
2. **Tax register:** AFM and TAXISnet codes, with the register showing you as **resident** at a Greek address.
3. **AMKA** (social security number) if you will be insured with EFKA.
4. **Keep a day count**, and deregister in your old country where required.
5. **Set aside Greek income tax monthly** from day one.
6. **File the E1** by 15 July of the following year, with foreign salary and any foreign tax declared.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I pay Greek tax if I work remotely in Greece for a foreign company?

Yes, once you are Greek tax resident: more than 183 days in Greece within any 12 months, or your main home or centre of life here. Your salary is then taxed in Greece on the normal 9% to 44% scale, because the work is physically done in Greece. Under the usual treaty rule, your employer's country generally loses the right to tax that salary.

### Does my foreign employer have to run Greek payroll?

For income tax, a foreign employer with no Greek establishment has no Greek withholding duty; you declare the salary in your annual E1 and pay the tax yourself. For social security it is different: if Greek EFKA applies, the employer must register with EFKA or agree with you that you pay the contributions on its behalf. Many employers use a Greek payroll provider or an Employer of Record instead.

### Do remote employees of a foreign company pay EFKA in Greece?

Usually yes. Under EU Regulation 883/2004 you are insured where you physically work, so full-time remote work from Greece means Greek EFKA. Greece has not signed the EU framework agreement on cross-border telework, so even partial telework from Greece gives no special route. Exceptions are a posting with an A1 certificate, normally up to 24 months, and work spread over several EU countries with less than 25% in Greece. For US, Canadian or Australian employers, the bilateral agreement decides.

### Do I pay advance tax in Greece on a foreign salary?

No. The Greek advance tax (prokatavoli) is calculated only on business income. Salary is not subject to it. But because nobody withholds Greek tax during the year, the full year's tax becomes due after you file the E1, payable in up to eight monthly instalments.

### Can I use Article 5C if my employer has no Greek entity?

Not under the employment route. Article 5C requires employment with a Greek legal entity or the Greek permanent establishment of a foreign company. You also need not to have been Greek tax resident in 5 of the previous 6 years. Digital Nomad Visa holders cannot use 5C at all.

### Can working from home in Greece create a permanent establishment for my employer?

It can. Greece follows the OECD rules: a home office the employer effectively requires you to use, over a longer period, can be a fixed place of business, and an employee who habitually negotiates or concludes contracts can create a dependent agent PE. Routine remote work without sales authority is lower risk, but the employer should assess it.
